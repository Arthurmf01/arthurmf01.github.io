Protecting the path into work

What happens to a career when AI takes over its first rung?

How AI is reshaping entry-level employment, and seven policies to protect the two places where skills are formed: education and the first job.

Arthur Froissart · Independent study supervised by Prof. Alexandra Roulet, INSEAD · October 2026

Read the full paper (PDF) → Skip to the proposals
  • 19% lower employment of 22 to 25 year olds in the occupations most exposed to AI than if they had tracked less exposed peers Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen, 2026
  • ~20% fall in employment of software developers aged 22 to 25 since the end of 2022 Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen, 2025
  • ~35% fewer entry-level job postings than in early 2023 Revelio Labs, 2026

Most of the debate on AI and work asks how many jobs AI will destroy or create. In a 2026 essay for CEPR, Philippe Aghion, Antonin Bergeaud, Simon Bunel and Alexandra Roulet argue that the fear of displacement is misplaced: the productivity gains from AI push firms to grow and to hire. The figures above point the other way, but only for one group. Older workers in the same occupations show no comparable decline. The squeeze is at the start of careers.

The paper's argument is that AI puts skill formation at risk before it puts jobs at risk. A student who uses AI to write an essay gets a good grade immediately. A firm that hands a junior's tasks to a model saves a salary immediately. In both cases the cost comes later, and it falls on someone else. So the question is how public policy can protect the formation of skills, in education and in the first job, without slowing the adoption of AI.

A running example

Follow one student, call her Léa. She is in the last year of her degree and aiming for a junior white-collar role, the kind of work where exposure to AI is highest. Léa is illustrative. The figures beside her are not.

Two words used throughout. The path into work is the two stages in which a young person builds the skills of a working life: education, then the first job. An entrant is a young person within two years of leaving initial education.

Part 1

The problem: AI is narrowing the path into work

1.1

AI already affects early-career jobs

Using payroll data from ADP, a large US payroll provider, Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen (2026) compare occupations by how exposed they are to AI. By June 2026, employment of 22 to 25 year olds in the most exposed occupations, such as software developers and customer service agents, was 19% below where it would have been had it followed their less exposed peers. More experienced workers in the same occupations show no comparable gap.

The effect falls on those starting out, not on those already in place.

1.1

France shows the same pattern

Between ChatGPT's launch in November 2022 and March 2026, permanent-contract (CDI) hires of 21 to 29 year olds fell by about 28% in the most AI-exposed occupations, against about 8% in the least exposed: some 6,200 fewer hires a month. For young software developers and IT engineers, the fall was about 42% (Palladino et al., 2026).

The French Treasury reports that 15 to 29 year olds contributed 3.8 points of decline to employment in IT services between the end of 2023 and the end of 2025 (Direction générale du Trésor, 2026).

Aside

What "exposed to AI" means, and why the figures disagree

An occupation is exposed when a large share of its tasks could be done or accelerated by a language model. Exposure measures what AI could do, not the jobs it actually removes. A job is a bundle of tasks, so AI can take over some of them without the job disappearing.

Three ways of measuring it give very different answers, from about a fifth of work to about three fifths of jobs. None of the three measures job loss.

1.2

A nuance

These figures need caution, and the CEPR authors remain cautious themselves. The decline coincides with the rise in interest rates and the end of Covid-era support, both of which also weighed on hiring. In France, the unemployment rate of 15 to 24 year olds rose from 19.1% to 21.1% between the first quarters of 2025 and 2026, a rise the Treasury links largely to the economic cycle.

Causality aside, the pattern matters. When firms adjust, junior hiring is the first lever they pull. And entry-level roles are not just jobs: they are where people learn the craft, through routine tasks, feedback and gradual responsibility.

1.3

At university: the grade now, the skill gap later

Léa asks a model to structure her case analysis. The grade arrives at once. What she skipped, learning to build the argument herself, only shows later, when she has to judge whether an AI-generated analysis holds up in front of a manager or a client.

The evidence bears this out. In a field experiment with nearly 1,000 Turkish high-school students, those given unrestricted access to GPT-4 scored 48% higher during practice, but 17% lower than peers who never had it once the tool was removed, while believing they had learned just as much (Bastani et al., 2025). In China, a study of nearly 27,000 pupils over 30 months found AI raised homework scores by 18% but lowered closed-book exam scores by 20% within six months (Strömberg, Lei and Wu, 2026).

A degree is meant to certify what a student can do alone. If grades measure what students produce with AI, the degree stops telling employers who has the skills.

1.4

In the first job: the salary saved now, the experience lost for a career

If Léa is hired, her first tasks would be drafting, summarising and checking data. These are exactly the tasks AI does best.

Daniel Dines, founder of UiPath, explains why through verifiability: AI improves fastest where its output can be checked at once, as a compiler confirms whether code works, and slowest where feedback comes late, as when an insurance claim approved today is proven wrong by a lawsuit two years later (Dines, 2026).

A model does those junior tasks in seconds for the price of a licence; a junior costs a salary and months of training. The choice may be rational for the firm even in the long run, because a model may one day do the senior's work too. That is the problem: the cost falls elsewhere, first on young people who lose the experience and the earnings that follow, then on a society that still needs people able to judge, correct and take responsibility for what models produce.

1.5

Why this is a public-policy problem

In both cases the actor behaves rationally: the student maximises the grade, the firm minimises its costs, and neither bears the long-term cost of the skills that are not formed. Each acts rationally; together they produce an outcome none would choose.

And the cost is invisible. No statistic records the job Léa was never offered.

When New York State added a checkbox in March 2025 asking firms filing notices of large layoffs whether AI was a reason, none of the more than 160 employers filing in the first year ticked it (Hunton Andrews Kurth, 2026). Asking firms to declare AI's role does not work. Policy must rely on data the state already collects: in France, every employer reports each hire, with age and contract type, every month through the DSN.

Part 2

Seven policies to protect skill formation

Taken in the order a career unfolds: first education, where skills are built, then the first job, where they are put to work. Some are national, others European. Each carries a caveat.

Recommendation 1 · Education · National

Foundations first: core skills are learned and tested without AI

Reading, writing, grammar, calculus and proof should first be practised without AI (Aghion et al., 2026), and assessed without it. France should define, for each level, a core of skills taught and assessed without AI, with handwritten exams as the default and orals at key points.

Others are already moving. Sweden has put more than SEK 2 billion since 2023 into school textbooks and kept early-grade national tests on paper. Princeton has made proctoring mandatory for in-person exams from July 2026.

Caveat. Employers want graduates fluent in AI, and timed handwritten tests disadvantage some students. The answer is sequence, not prohibition, with accommodations kept for pupils who need them.

Recommendation 2 · Education · National

Let AI into the classroom gradually, with the teacher in charge

The core is a supervised gateway: at defined points, an oral, a handwritten exam or a laptop-free exercise certifies what the student can do alone. France should pilot a guided tutor, with a frontier or sovereign model, and a randomised evaluation built in before any scaling.

The closest example is Estonia's AI Leap, which started on 1 September 2025 with 20,000 pupils in grades 10 and 11. Teachers were trained first. Pupils got a learning app that guides them towards answers rather than giving them, and researchers at the University of Tartu are measuring the effect on learning.

Caveat. Cost. A national roll-out has not been costed; the largest item is likely teacher training rather than computing. Estonia's published indicators so far cover take-up, not learning.

Recommendation 3 · Education · National

A guided AI tutor for every pupil until they master the core

Progression would depend on mastery, checked in supervised exams without AI. A pupil who falls short would not repeat the year: they would keep working with the tutor on the missing skills while moving on elsewhere.

Mastery learning has long been one of the most effective methods in education (Bloom, 1984). Its limit was the cost of one tutor per pupil. Well-designed AI tutors now come close.

Caveat. Holding pupils back on its own does not work (Goos et al., 2021). Mastery must come with remediation, not repetition.

Recommendation 4 · First job · National

A training credit that follows the entrant to a first employer

The state should credit the training account (CPF) of every entrant without a first job: a graduate who has never worked starts with a zero balance. The entrant spends it on a course with an independently assessed result, the provider paid in full only on that result, or transfers it to a first employer offering a training-heavy junior contract.

The credit goes to the young person because today's support starts only once a firm has hired. Since March 2026 the apprenticeship aid has fallen to €2,000 for small firms and €750 for large ones for apprentices above Bac+2, the profiles most exposed to AI.

Caveat. Training effects are real but modest: 8 to 9 points more employment two years after a qualifying course, no wage gain, and a method its authors say probably biases the result upwards.

Recommendation 5 · First job · National, coordinated in Europe

A low tax on firms' AI spending, earmarked for entrants

Labour is taxed through payroll contributions; software is not, so a firm that replaces a person with a model also cuts its tax bill (Faivre and Cen, 2026). Of the designs Faivre and Cen analyse, the paper chooses a low tax on what firms spend on AI services: spending is invoiced, replacement is not.

The tax would be national, since an EU tax requires unanimity, with Europe coordinating the base and a minimum rate through the existing reverse-charge mechanism for cross-border services. It is a first step in a debate that is coming, not a final answer.

Caveat. Taxing AI use raises adoption costs in a Europe already behind. A low rate limits the risk but does not remove it.

Recommendation 6 · First job · European

Junior-hiring reporting on the carbon model: scopes 1, 2 and 3

The problem resembles climate change. A firm that replaces workers saves on wages, but those workers then spend less, which hurts other firms' sales; no firm pays for that loss, so all automate more than is good for the economy as a whole (Falk and Tsoukalas, 2026). The cost is also deferred, and it can be moved: a firm can cut juniors on its payroll and buy the same work from a contractor.

Climate policy solved that with scopes, so an emission moved down the chain still counts. Each figure would be compared with the firm's pre-2023 average and its sector median. The vehicle exists: the largest EU firms already report on their own workforce and value-chain workers under the CSRD. The junior share could simply be added.

Caveat. The EU has just cut CSRD datapoints by more than 70% and taken most firms out of scope, and scope 3 is hard to measure. A first version should start with scopes 1 and 2.

Recommendation 7 · First job · National, coordinated in Europe

Hire juniors or pay for their training

Large firms below their junior-hiring benchmark should either hire enough entrants to meet it or pay a contribution equal to the cost of training a junior elsewhere, priced so that paying is never cheaper than training. The firm chooses; the state never asks what technology it uses.

A hiring subsidy alone would not do: France's 2020 youth hiring aid moved 22 to 25 year olds into longer contracts without raising their total employment (DARES, 2023).

Caveat. Firms may relabel jobs as junior, which is why an entrant is defined by time since leaving education, not by job title. And as the rule works, contributions shrink, so they cannot be a stable source of funding.

Conclusion

AI puts skill formation at risk before it puts jobs at risk

The benefits of AI come immediately and go to the individual student or firm. The costs come later and fall on society as a whole. The answer is to protect the two places where skills are formed, education and the first job, and to tie public action to results the state can actually measure: test scores, AI spending and the number of juniors hired.

Apart from the small cost of the tax in Recommendation 5, none of these measures would slow the adoption of AI. Their purpose is to make sure the generation growing up with AI does not merely use it, but remains able to judge, correct and improve what it produces.

For Léa, that means a degree that still certifies what she can do alone, and a first job that still exists to learn in.
